ABA Formal Opinion 512, Translated Into Controls for AI Agents

In July 2024 the American Bar Association told lawyers that the Rules of Professional Conduct apply to generative AI. Here's what that means when your firm puts an AI agent to work, duty by duty, with the controls that match.

Short answer

ABA Formal Opinion 512 applies the existing Rules of Professional Conduct to generative AI. For an AI agent, that means testing it before live matters, limiting its access to client data, deciding when to tell clients, supervising its output, verifying anything filed, and billing only real time. A seven-point policy starter is below.

A practical guide, and not legal advice. Your ethics counsel and your state's rules have the final word. We build the agents. You decide what's permitted.

What Does ABA Formal Opinion 512 Say?

On July 29, 2024, the ABA's Standing Committee on Ethics and Professional Responsibility issued Formal Opinion 512, "Generative Artificial Intelligence Tools." It's the ABA's first formal opinion on lawyers' use of generative AI.

The core message is simple. The rules you already follow still apply. The opinion works through six areas: competence, confidentiality, communication with clients, supervision, candor to the tribunal, and fees.

An AI agent doesn't change those duties. It changes where you have to apply them.

What Should a Firm Do With an AI Agent, Duty by Duty?

Competence (Model Rule 1.1)

Lawyers need a reasonable understanding of the benefits and risks of the technology they use. The opinion also warns that relying on a tool's output without appropriate independent verification can fall short of the competence duty.

  • Test every agent on real, past matters before it touches a live one.
  • Require attorney review for anything that carries legal judgment.
  • Keep a log of what the agent produced and who approved it.

Confidentiality (Model Rule 1.6)

Client information has to stay protected, including when it's entered into an AI tool. The opinion points lawyers to the risk that information is disclosed or accessed by others, and to reviewing a tool's terms and privacy practices.

  • Give each agent access only to the matters and systems it needs.
  • Review the terms and data handling of every tool and model the agent uses.
  • Decide in writing what client data may enter an agent, and what may not.

Communication (Model Rule 1.4)

Lawyers have to keep clients reasonably informed, which includes thinking about when AI use should be disclosed.

  • Decide when your firm tells clients about AI use, and put that language in your engagement letter.
  • Answer client questions about how agents are used with a consistent, honest script.

Supervision (Model Rules 5.1 and 5.3)

Managerial lawyers are expected to set clear firm policies on permissible uses of generative AI and to supervise how staff use it.

  • Write an AI use policy and train everyone on it.
  • Treat agent output the way you'd treat a junior colleague's work: reviewed before it goes out.

Candor to the tribunal (Model Rules 3.1 and 3.3)

Anything filed with a court has to be accurate and verified, whatever drafted it.

  • Verify every citation and quotation independently.
  • Keep agents out of the final filing step. A person signs.

Fees (Model Rule 1.5)

Fees must stay reasonable. The ABA's example: if a lawyer spends 15 minutes entering information into a tool to draft a pleading, the lawyer may bill that time and the time spent reviewing the draft. In most circumstances a lawyer can't bill a client for the time it takes to learn how to use the tool.

  • Bill the time you actually spend, including review.
  • Don't bill clients for learning a tool.

What Should New York Lawyers Know?

New York lawyers have more to read. The New York State Bar Association's Task Force on Artificial Intelligence published its report and recommendations in April 2024, covering the legal, social, and ethical impact of AI on the profession. The Association of the City of New York Bar published a May 2025 compilation of current ethics opinions and reports on generative AI across jurisdictions.

Check your own state's rules and any court-specific orders before you deploy anything.

What Goes in a Seven-Point AI Policy?

  1. Approved tools. A short list of tools and models the firm has reviewed.
  2. Data rules. What client information may enter an agent, and what may not.
  3. Review rule. Which outputs need attorney approval before they're used.
  4. Client disclosure. When and how you tell clients.
  5. Billing rule. How AI-assisted work is recorded and billed.
  6. Logging. A record of what each agent did, and who approved it.
  7. Training and review. Onboarding for new staff and an annual refresh as the tools change.
