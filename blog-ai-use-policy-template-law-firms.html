A Free AI Use Policy Template for Law Firms

ABA Formal Opinion 512 expects firms to set clear rules for AI. Here's a ten-section policy you can copy or download, with the reasoning behind each part.

Short answer

A law firm AI use policy needs ten sections: scope, approved tools, data rules, attorney review, AI agents, client consent, billing, logging, incidents, and training. Below is a template you can copy or download as a Word file, built around the duties in ABA Formal Opinion 512.

What Does a Law Firm AI Use Policy Need to Cover?

A good policy answers the questions a partner, an associate, or a state bar investigator would ask: which tools, what data, who checks the output, what clients are told, how time is billed, and what gets logged. Each section ties back to a duty in Opinion 512.

Approved tools, data rulesCompetence and confidentiality1.1, 1.6
Attorney reviewCompetence and candor to the tribunal1.1, 3.1, 3.3

Why Does a Firm Need a Written Policy at All?

Opinion 512 expects managerial lawyers to set clear policies on permissible uses of generative AI and to supervise how staff use it. A written policy turns that expectation into something you can train on and audit.

The cost of skipping verification is public. In Mata v. Avianca, two New York lawyers filed a brief citing cases that ChatGPT had invented, and on June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel imposed a $5,000 penalty on the lawyers and their firm.

The Template

Copy it into your firm's format, or download the Word version.

[Firm Name] Generative AI Use Policy

Effective date: [date]  |  Owner: [Role]  |  Review: every 12 months and when tools or rules change

1. Purpose and scope

This policy sets the rules for using generative AI at [Firm Name], including chatbots, drafting assistants, and AI agents. It applies to every attorney, paralegal, staff member, contractor, and vendor who works on firm matters.

It supports our duties under the Rules of Professional Conduct, including competence, confidentiality, communication, supervision, candor to the tribunal, and reasonable fees.

2. Approved tools

Only tools on the Approved Tools List may be used with firm or client information. [Role, e.g., the General Counsel or Managing Partner] keeps the list.

A tool is added only after review of its terms, data handling, security, and retention. Personal accounts and unapproved tools may not be used for firm work. To request a tool, [process].

3. Data rules

Level 1, never entered into any AI tool: [e.g., sealed or court-protected material, government ID numbers, bank account numbers, health records].

Level 2, entered only into approved tools configured so our data is not used to train models: client confidential information and attorney work product.

Level 3, allowed in any approved tool: public information and firm marketing material.

4. Attorney review and verification

A lawyer is responsible for everything produced with AI help. Before using AI output, the responsible lawyer must verify every citation, quotation, and factual claim against a primary source, and review the draft for accuracy, completeness, and tone.

AI output may not be filed with a court or sent to a client without attorney review. A person signs every filing.

5. AI agents

An AI agent that takes actions, such as sending messages, updating records, or moving files, needs: a named owner; a written list of what it may access and do; least-privilege access; human approval before it sends anything to a client, files anything, or changes billing; a log of every action; and a way to switch it off immediately.

6. Client communication and consent

Our engagement letter describes how we use AI in client matters. We obtain informed consent where the Rules or the client's instructions require it. Attorneys answer client questions about AI use honestly.

[Role] reviews the engagement-letter language every [12 months].

7. Billing

We bill for time actually spent, including time spent reviewing AI output. We do not bill clients for time spent learning an AI tool. [Describe any flat-fee or per-task arrangements.]

8. Logging and records

We record which tools were used on which matters, who used them, and who reviewed the output. Agent logs are kept for [period] and retained under our records policy.

9. Incidents

Report any suspected leak of client information, any AI error that reached a client or a court, and any tool misuse to [Role] within [24 hours].

[Role] decides, with ethics counsel, whether to notify the client, the court, or our insurance carrier.

10. Training, ownership, and review

Everyone completes AI training when they join and every [12 months]. [Role] owns this policy and reviews it at least once a year, and whenever tools, court rules, or ethics guidance change.

Violations are handled under [firm discipline policy].

Acknowledgment

I have read and will follow this policy. Name: ____________   Date: ____________

How Do You Roll the Policy Out?

  1. Name an owner. One person is accountable for the policy and the tool list.
  2. Inventory what's already in use. Ask everyone, with amnesty, which AI tools they've tried. Shadow use is common.
  3. Approve a short list. Review terms and data handling, then publish the list.
  4. Train and acknowledge. A 30-minute session and a signed acknowledgment.
  5. Spot-check quarterly. Sample a few matters, confirm the log exists, and confirm review happened.

How Do AI Agents Change the Policy?

A chatbot answers questions. An agent acts: it sends messages, updates records, and moves files. Section 5 of the template covers the extra controls an agent needs, namely a named owner, scoped access, approval gates, a log, and an off switch.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a law firm need a written AI policy?

ABA Formal Opinion 512 expects managerial lawyers to set clear firm policies on permissible uses of generative AI and to supervise how staff use it. Whether your state requires the policy in writing depends on its rules, so check with your state bar. A written policy is the simplest way to show you did it.

Can a lawyer bill a client for time spent using AI?

Yes, for the time actually spent. The ABA gives this example: if a lawyer spends 15 minutes entering information into a tool to draft a pleading, the lawyer may bill that time and the time spent reviewing the draft. In most circumstances a lawyer can't bill a client for the time it takes to learn how to use the tool.

Do we have to tell clients we use AI?

Rule 1.4 requires reasonable consultation with the client about the means used to reach their objectives, and Rule 1.6 protects client information unless the client gives informed consent. Many firms address AI in the engagement letter. Your state's guidance may say more, so check it.

What happened in Mata v. Avianca?

Two New York lawyers filed a brief that cited cases ChatGPT had invented. On June 22, 2023, Judge P. Kevin Castel of the Southern District of New York imposed a $5,000 penalty on the lawyers and their firm, and the case is a widely cited example of why attorney verification belongs in every AI policy.
